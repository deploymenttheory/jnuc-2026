JNUC 2026 / Sandbox / ClickOps to GitOps / new slide, round 3

Mindset shift - three states, on the two-tone split

Round three. The visual is settled: option B from round two, the two grounds with the hard edge down the middle, the navy panel carrying the title. Every option below is built on it. What changes is the content model - the shift is no longer two states but three: the old mindset, the question the engineer is asking themselves, and the GitOps mindset that replaced it. The middle is the new part, and it is the part that makes the other two mean anything: nobody crossed from one column to the other without asking these first.

The wording

The middle column is written as the questions an engineer asks themselves on the way across, not as statements about them. That is the difference between describing resistance and showing it: a question puts the room inside the engineer's head, and every one of these has an answer sitting in the column to its right. Each is anchored in a line this deck already carries rather than invented.

  • The console is where I work → How do I fix it at 3am? → The repo is where I work.
    The honest question, and the deck already answers it twice: slide 3 keeps breakglass GUI access as a legitimate call, and slide 11 builds a whole document type around "it's broken and I need a fact now", in those words, at 3am.

  • I tell it what to do → I understood the slides. What do I do when I open VS Code? → I describe what should be true.
    Slide 5's engineer quote turned round into the question it was really asking: "I understood the slides but had no idea what to actually do when I opened VS Code."

  • I fix it now → Why does my change need someone else's approval? → I raise it, and someone reviews it.
    Slide 14's warning is that this reads as "process for its own sake" unless someone names what it buys them; condition 07 on slide 7 is the answer, deciding who is qualified to gate a change.

  • I am the expert here → If my Jamf Pro experience does not count here, what does? → I am a beginner again.
    Slide 5 answers it flatly: "Prior coding experience predicted success, not Jamf Pro familiarity or age." The hardest question on the slide, and the one with the least comfortable answer.

  • A mistake is a risk → What happens the day I break something that matters? → A mistake is recoverable.
    Slide 7 asks it in the same words: psychological safety "has to still hold the week somebody breaks something that matters".

  • This will make me faster → How long am I going to feel this slow? → This makes me slower, first.
    Condition 06 gives the number: expect the dip, two to three months before people were comfortable raising and reviewing PRs. Say it before they ask.

The closing line from the earlier rounds is gone. With a question column doing the work of saying why this was hard, it was restating the slide rather than adding to it. Say if you want it back.

Still open: the position (recommended new slide 8, after conditions and before priorities by role) and who presents it.

Option A - Three columns, three states

The most literal reading of the brief: the navy panel carries the ClickOps mindset, a narrow middle column carries the concern in the deck's warning colour, and the lime column carries the GitOps mindset. All six rows, all three states, and the two-tone split still falls after the first column so the accepted visual is intact. The row grid means each concern sits level with the belief it belongs to, which is what makes it read as a passage rather than three separate lists. Densest of the five.
